### Before you read: what the words mean

A **unit** is one of the 11,008 units in a layer's MLP. It computes one signed number per token, its activation. The
tokens where that number is positive are the unit's **positive side**; the tokens where it is negative are its
**negative side**. Most measurements below are made on a side, not on a unit, because the two sides respond to
different inputs. A **snapshot** is the model's weights saved at one training step. A side's **fifty contexts** are the
fifty positions in a fixed corpus of 20.6 million tokens where its activation was largest at the final snapshot. A
**group** is a set of those fifty contexts that a separate model sorted together by shared content. A group's
**birth** is the first snapshot at which the side's activation on that group's positions crosses a threshold defined
in chapter 0 and stays above it at the next snapshot. The side whose group is born is the **carrier**. A **job** is a
group that an explanation written from its contexts predicts on new text.

## 1. Fifty contexts, sixteen snapshots

**19 to 25 October.** At the end of training, does a side respond to one recognizable kind of input? And if its fifty
contexts fall into several groups, did the later groups grow out of the earlier ones, or did unrelated groups pack into
the same unit?

We harvested the sixteen snapshots for 600 sides of 300 units. A separate model sorted each side's fifty contexts into
groups and named them. For each group we wrote an explanation and tested it on new texts. We dated each group's birth
by the rule in chapter 0. For each group born late, a judge model took the parent test: shown the group and two
records from the snapshot before its birth, could it tell which record belonged to the group's own side? Before the
run we wrote down what we expected: growth from earlier groups 0.65, a mixture 0.30, packing 0.05.

[picture: neuron_across_training] "One unit's fifty contexts across the sixteen snapshots."

[picture: births] "When each group was born, against random groups of the same sizes drawn from the same fifty: the
real groups' first-born share is 0.476, the random groups' 0.530."

[table: chapter 1] one job, three models; the parent test and its control; predictions against outcomes; the judge's
first two controls

The results landed on the branches we had weighted least. On OLMo-3 7B, 51.8 % of sides had one job under an
explanation that passed its test (band 47.8 to 55.8 %); the warning line we had written in advance, that the essays'
90 % would not appear, fired on all three models we tried. The parent test gave an unrelated share of 0.265 (band 0.211
to 0.321), above the 0.20 line, so by the rule written in advance the result is packing, the branch we had given 0.05.
{journal: already-clean JOURNAL 2026-10-23 20:02Z} Two tools failed before the run could count: the first sorter failed
its agreement check, and the first judge failed its control twice before a second judge passed at 0.910.

One reading did point toward growth: the side's strongest group at the end of training was also its first born in
0.476 of units. On 5 November a check showed that random groups of the same sizes are first born more often, 0.530, so
that reading is not supported and is marked so wherever it appears. {journal: 2026-11-05 09:58 CST} A red team found
the judge's control easier than its test items; adjusted for that, the unrelated share is 0.206, at the line. We
withdrew the words "phase change". Two defects in the explainer were found on 25 October and not re-run, and the study
closed.

Open: why random groups are first born so often; a harder control for the judge could not be measured (0.798 against
the 0.80 required), so "the judge separates by domain alone" is not ruled out; and the plan's own rule for a packing
outcome, to demote the unit as the object of study, which no file records as applied or set aside.

## 2. Seven small studies

**25 October to 4 November.** If the groups were not growing from one another in the text, were they connected in the
wiring? Was the moment of birth anything at all? And were our editing tools measuring what they claimed?

Seven small studies. Three on single units in other models ended without a result that counted; one comparison was
cancelled when its own check failed for 42 % and 37 % of units. A wiring score read 0.356 against 0.159 for fake units
made by joining two unrelated ones, but it hardly changed when computed for a different unit, and it was left open.
Replacing the older group's 50 input units with their average lowered the unit on the newer group: 0.226 against 0.174
for fake units at the end of training, so under the rule written first the reuse reading was refuted; against fake
units at the birth point (0.031) it read as reuse. In the saved data we found the always-on units: the 1 % of
units with the largest mean activation over ordinary text, 3,412 of them, which carry about two thirds of an unrelated
unit's input effect. From 2 November they were excluded from the input-replacement method. They do not act through the
layer's normalization; a sentence from 2 November was withdrawn. The moment of birth could not be read: one snapshot
before the birth the unit already responded at 0.95 of its birth level; the activation ratio rises through all of
training, and the sharpest change sits near the birth in 0.307 of cases against 0.283 for shuffled births. The older
group was not preferred over another group of the same unit (−0.046, band −0.091 to 0.030). In a published edit of
another model, 11 of 128 edited units were always-on against a line of 32, and the edit test could not be read because
193 to 200 of 200 answers ran to the length limit. Grouping contexts by their input profiles, without reading text,
agreed with the sorter at 0.25. Last, the editing tools against a known edit: under the rule frozen in advance every
reading was cancelled, because the direction had been taken from the published edit itself.

The position on 4 November: a unit's groups use inputs they hold in common, whichever came first; the birth of a group

## 4. Three data mixes

**5 November.** Does changing the training data move which documents hold a side's strongest contexts?

AI2 trained three continuations of 100 billion tokens from one stage-1 snapshot: 0 %, 40 % and 65 % code and
mathematics. For every one of the 704,512 sides we measured the share of its fifty strongest corpus positions that
fall in the five code-and-mathematics directories, before and after each mix, paired by side, and summarized the mean
over each band of eight layers. Before reading, three checks passed: 1,535 of 1,535 banked activations reproduced bit
for bit; the drift between neighbouring stage-1 snapshots set the bar a difference had to clear; the start snapshot
harvested twice gave byte-identical counts.

[picture: f12] "Per band of eight layers, the code-and-mathematics share of a side's fifty at the start and after each
mix; the drift band is the difference between neighbouring stage-1 snapshots."

The mix with no code or mathematics moved the share by 0.6 to 0.9 of one of the fifty per side, mostly away from those
documents. The 65 % mix held it within 0.36 of one. The ordering we predicted, 0 below 40 below 65, held at the
resolution of the resampling band but not at the resolution of ordinary stage-1 drift, so it is undecided. By the
spec's own decision table the next step for this outcome was to report, not to run a per-side analysis. {journal:
m1/READ_head.md 2026-11-05 17:40 CST} The size was corrected on 8 November, from "about one of the fifty" to 0.6 to 0.9,
by the reader who checked the abstract. {journal: 2026-11-08 05:54 CST}

Open: whether a mix moves which token family dominates a side's strongest contexts, a reading prompted by a preprint we
read that afternoon; two fixes to the measure before it is reused.

## 5. Naming the carrier before the birth

**5 to 6 November.** One snapshot before a group's birth, can its carrier be picked out of its layer? And is the carrier
easier to pick than a random side would be for a random group?

For each of the 153 births, we scored all 22,016 sides of the layer at the snapshot before the birth (the rule in
chapter 0, picture 7) and recorded the carrier's rank. The rules were set on the 82 births of even-index units and the
71 births of odd-index units were then read once. The control was a random group of another side's own fifty, of the
same size, dated to the same snapshot by the same rule, scored the same way for its own side. Six attempts ran. Three
stopped at a check that compared two devices; one crashed on an assertion; one stopped because the input that was
supposed to fail its check had no power to. The device check became a report rather than a stop: a threshold which
cannot be frozen on control data is a measurement, not a check. {journal: 2026-11-05 22:20 CST} A program rule
followed: the offline check evaluates both sides of every check on banked controls before launch. {journal: 2026-11-06
00:50 CST}

[picture: f10] "The carrier is in the top ten of 22,016 sides in 42 of 71 held-out births; a random group of another
side's contexts puts its own side in the top ten in 53 of 70."

The carrier is named at about 1,300 times chance, and the control is named more often: the difference is −0.171, band
−0.286 to −0.057. The raw examples showed why: other sides of the layer already held the group's positions one snapshot
before. The record's next step: the competition study moved up. {journal: 2026-11-06 02:20 CST} The reader of this
study found, after the data were seen, that the gap sits on births whose contexts repeat one token (carrier named in
22 against 53 of 78) and not on births with varied tokens (57 against 58 of 71); a control matched on the number of
distinct tokens was registered as the follow-up. {journal: 2026-11-06 02:40 CST}

Left open, and taken up next: does the carrier rise on its own or do the others fall; does the distinct-token control
close the gap; does the gradient one snapshot earlier point at the carrier.

## 6. The gradient, and a measurement that could not resolve

**6 November, 03:50 to 10:00.** Three questions on the same 153 births, and a condition: the gradient's answer would
count only if a first-order prediction of switching a side off agreed with the real switch-off at rank agreement 0.7,
a bar raised from 0.5 before the run.

The first attempt did not count because the backward pass was not bit-deterministic (4 of 98 units flipped). The
second ran in 16-bit, with reduced-precision attention to fit the GPU's memory. {journal: 2026-11-06 04:45, 07:35 CST}

[picture: f09, state 1] "The 16-bit prediction against the 16-bit measurement of switching off: rank agreement 0.100 on
the rule-setting half and 0.043 on the held-out half, against a bar of 0.7."

Two of the three questions were answered. The carrier wins by its own rise (0.951, band 0.738 to 1.180), and the reader
found that the dating rule nearly forces that sign. The distinct-token control closes the gap: carrier in the top ten in
42 of 71, control in 41 of 67 (difference −0.030, band −0.149 to +0.090). The third did not count: the validation
failed, and the gradient readings were printed as exploratory. {journal: 2026-11-06 07:35 CST}

The reader's finding at 08:10 changed what we thought had failed. The check had measured the resolution of a 16-bit
window-summed switch-off measurement, not the gradient. The measured change was 27 times the prediction at the median,
with no relation to it. An earlier reading, "first-order breakdown", was withdrawn. The decision: build the switch-off
measurement in 32-bit, and make the weight change behind the carrier's rise the next object. {journal: 2026-11-06 08:10
CST} The user's rule the same morning: a job whose memory exceeds the GPU runs on a larger GPU elsewhere, not under a
precision compromise. {journal: 2026-11-06 10:00 CST}

Open: whether the 20 sides that scored highest one snapshot earlier fall (undecided after the re-read in chapter 8); a
switch-off of those 20 sides, about one GPU-hour, not run.

16:44Z}

[picture: f11] "For 67 held-out births, the carrier's weight-change statistic against a matched unit's: the mean

[picture: f09, state 2] "The same 16-bit prediction against the 32-bit measurement: rank agreement 0.960 on 284 held-out

The carrier's input rows changed less, not more. The output column read −0.258 (band −0.654 to 0.130); the

## 9. Where the mathematics gain lives

**5 to 8 November, alongside chapters 4 to 8.** The first pick. When the finished model is trained further on
mathematics, is the gain in a few units?

The offline check found that the grading rule judged 108 of 500 answers unequal to themselves; it was fixed before the
rules were frozen. {journal: 2026-11-05 10:15 CST} The published score took seven launches to reproduce (39.96 against
40.0 ± 1.6). A smoke run showed no condition would fit under the 35 GPU-hour ceiling, and the user removed the limits.
{journal: 2026-11-05 21:45 CST} The first ranking run showed the change was flat across layers, so layer-window
conditions were retired. {journal: 2026-11-05 23:45 CST} The random units meant to match the most-changed units' energy
realized only 0.70 to 0.82 of it after 16-bit rounding; stochastic rounding fixed it. {journal: 2026-11-06 05:00 CST}
The reference run read base 57.2, mathematics model 92.0, zeroed change 55.4, identity 91.2; the gain of 34.8 cleared
the bound of 13.60. {journal: 2026-11-06 12:15 CST} A 20-hour outage followed, and two launches failed on an
exact-equality check while nobody was watching; a tolerance was added. {journal: 2026-11-07 09:20 CST} Then the
conditions ran, each checked by tensor hashes and engine equality against the reference run.

[picture: f08] "Each condition's score on 500 competition problems: copy the 0.1 % of units that changed most and the
score is 57.6 against the base's 57.2; copy a random 0.1 % of the MLP's changed entries, scaled up a thousandfold, and
it is 85.2 of the mathematics model's 92.0."

[table: chapter 9] every condition and its score; the held-out word problems; the noise of one reading

The most-changed 0.1, 0.3, 1 and 3 % of units read 57.6, 61.4, 61.8 and 63.8; the 3 % recovers 6.6 of the 34.8 points.
Random units of equal change energy read 58.0 to 63.8, the same. A random 0.1 % of the MLP's changed entries, scaled
up to estimate the whole change, read 85.2. All MLP read 86.4; attention with every norm weight 83.0. On 1,319 held-out
word problems the three comparisons at 1 % all replicated; the most-changed units minus the scaled sample read −23.7
(band −26.5 to −21.1). The decision, in the words the abstract's reader made us use: no 0.1 to 3 % subset of units carries the gain;
a sparse unbiased estimate of the whole MLP change does. {journal: 2026-11-08 05:00, 05:31 CST} The scaled sample is
matched on count, not on energy (its energy is 33 to 1,000 times the change's), so "spread out or just scaled" is open.
A proposal, made on 7 November, to add the most-changed 1 % to another post-trained model was withdrawn on 8 November;
the withdrawal is recorded in the handoff and nowhere earlier. {journal: HANDOFF.md 2026-11-08}

Open: the shape of the change that the network tolerates such noise on (a rank test is the named next measurement,
not yet specified or launched); whether the noise tolerance is an artifact (indeterminate); the MLP-against-attention
gap sits in answers that hit the length cap; the small word-problem contrasts are fragile.

## 10. Does an older side cost more to edit?

**6 to 8 November.** The fifth pick. Take two sides in one layer, one born early and one born late, matched on
activation size and density. Remove a fixed fraction of each side's write along its own column (chapter 0, picture
12). Does the older one cost more in loss?

The study pre-registered 74 matched pairs; after matching, age and activation size were uncorrelated (+0.027, from
−0.592 before). {journal: 2026-11-06 09:10 CST} The first attempt failed its side check because two batch shapes round
differently; the second gave 45 balanced pairs and failed two checks on two-path rounding. A power preview at 96
windows showed the resampling spread exceeded the contrasts, so the evaluation set went to 1,024 windows, a power check
was added and paid first, the positive control was trimmed to 8 layers, and the run was split in two halves, the second
held behind chapter 9. {journal: 2026-11-06 09:40, 10:30, 11:00 to 11:55 CST} The first half launched on 7 November,
before chapter 9's result, rather than hold the GPU idle; the positive control ran inside it. {journal: 2026-11-07 14:05
CST}

[picture: f13] "Per layer, the loss cost and the shared-vector change of the own-direction edit and of a random edit of
the same size: on the 8 control sides the random edit moved the shared vector 1.3 to 2.5 times more and cost more in 6
of 8 layers; on the 21 pairs the two edits moved it alike."

The control ran backwards. The own-direction edit cost more than the random edit in 2 of 8 layers, where 6 were needed,
and the random edit moved the shared vector 1.3 to 2.5 times more on every control side; on the 21 pairs the two edits
moved it alike (60.8 against 60.7). The power check failed too: the noise of a pair difference (its resampling spread over windows), 1.51e-6 nats per
token, was 1.186 times half the median pair difference, where it had to be at most 1. An exploratory reading exists (the early side's
edit cost more in 16 of 21 pairs) and is not a claim. The decision on 8 November: the frozen rule decides; the second
half would buy a result the rule had already declared does not count; a larger evaluation set is lower leverage than
the rank test of chapter 9. The design rule the study left, recorded as a finding and not yet as a spec rule: match
controls on their effect, not on their size in the weights. {journal: 2026-11-08 09:15 CST}

Open: the question itself, whether an older side costs more to edit; the noise floor, which no second reader
recounted.
