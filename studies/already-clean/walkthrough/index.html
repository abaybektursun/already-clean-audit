What we found

Six results, each measured. The rest of the page is the evidence.

  1. One neuron added what it fires on in a chain that can be read token by token. A layer-3 neuron of OLMo-3-7B fired on “Got” at the start of a clause by step 5,000 of training, added “gotta” and “gonna” by step 20,000, the piece “ot” inside “got” by 35,000 and “gettin’” by 100,000; for each addition, a judge shown the neuron’s earlier strongest contexts picked them over a random neuron’s in 4 of 4 calls A_training_dynamics.md §5.
  2. About a quarter of the later additions cannot be traced to the neuron’s past. Across 1,238 groups of contexts that appeared after a neuron’s first, the judge could not trace a share of 0.265 [0.211, 0.321] to the neuron’s earlier record. These groups sit mostly on neurons whose groups all appear late; inside one neuron, later groups are traced as well as earlier ones (299 pairs against 309) already-clean RESULTS.md, Q2; E_skeptic.md §3.1.
  3. A neuron’s groups share wiring. On 91 fresh cases, two groups of one neuron share 0.120 [0.080, 0.160] of the 50 earlier neurons that feed them most, where two unrelated neurons share 0.06 or less. Switching off one group’s 50 inputs lowers the neuron on the other group by a quarter to a third of what switching off its own does, and on groups defined from the wiring alone the overlap of the two input sets predicts that fall with a correlation of 0.87 reuse RESULTS.md Results 29, 53 to 58; instruments RESULTS.md Results 7, 21.
  4. A flaw in a standard tool, found and fixed. A few dozen mid-layer OLMo neurons have average activations about 145 times their layer’s typical value, so switching neurons off by writing in their average disturbs every later neuron through them. Excluding those neurons and writing zero lowers the score a fake neuron, made by adding two unrelated neurons, reaches by chance from 0.384 to 0.210, and the real cases do not move instruments RESULTS.md Results 6 to 9.
  5. A language model’s groups follow the marked token. When a language model sorts a neuron’s strongest contexts into groups, it rarely puts one marked token in two groups (in its first sorting, 383 of 18,148 neuron-and-token pairs, on 196 of the 600 neurons), so the token alone predicts a held-out context’s group 74% of the time. Yet 1,964 of its 2,454 groups hold more than one token, and the neuron’s own inputs see the split about as well as the token does instruments RESULTS.md Results 12 to 14; the sorting model’s record.
  6. Some neurons have a buried past. One neuron’s 20 strongest contexts were 20 of 20 “ lithium” at step 60,000 and 19 of 20 at step 320,000; by the end it had lost the top 20 to the endings of famous names, some of which were already above its line while lithium held the top. A description written from the final contexts alone cannot see this A_training_dynamics.md §2; E_skeptic.md §3.4.

Every wiring number is from one model, OLMo-3-7B, at 16 saved points of training; the flaw in the fourth result does not appear in the two other models tested. The groups come from a language model’s sorting, so the wiring results hold over the cases as a group, not case by case.

An essay says most neurons have one job

In 2026, Nick Cammarata of Goodfire published the essay “Neural Networks Are Already Clean”. This study tests it.

A neuron is polysemantic when it seems to respond to several unrelated things. The leading account was superposition: a network packs unrelated things into one neuron because they rarely occur together. If packing were common, a neuron could not be read directly; a second model, a decomposition method, would first have to separate the packed parts.

The essay argues that packing is rare, and that most apparent mixtures come from weak tools, flawed model designs and recursive generalization: a neuron’s job extends to related cases, which extend again, as in a neuron that fired on “iy”, then “diy”, then “do it yourself”. From preliminary audits of Qwen3-32B it estimates that about 90% of neurons have one main job, about 7% two related jobs, and 2–4% are not yet understood essay, The Short Version. It says the way to tell growth from packing is to watch neurons during training, which its authors could not do for a large model essay, Is Recursive Generalization Hard to Vary?.

Background · tokens, neurons, activations
Token
A word or a piece of a word, the unit a language model reads.
Neuron
Each layer of these language models has an MLP block, a set of neurons that each compute one number per token.
Activation
That number. It can be positive or negative.
Two sides
The study treats a neuron’s positive values and its negative values separately, and this page counts each side as one neuron, so the 300 neurons drawn from a model count as 600. L03N7155+ means layer 3, neuron 7155, positive side.
Corpus
About 50 million tokens of OLMo-3’s training text, which every model reads with its own tokenizer. The explanations and their test scores come from a scoring of all of it at the end of training; each of the 16 training points was scored on its first 20,560,068 tokens.

The 16 training points

OLMo-3-1025-7B saved 1,421 copies of its weights in its first stage of training, one every 1,000 steps of about 4.19 million tokens, and the study uses 16 of them, packed into the start spec §4.1, §4.3.

One neuron’s 50 contexts, sorted into groups

Before the study could ask whether a neuron’s later groups grew out of its earlier ones, it needed the groups. A language model, Claude Sonnet 5.5, made them. For each of the 600 neurons it read the neuron’s 50 strongest contexts and sorted them by what the neuron responds to spec §2.4.

What the model was given

One request per neuron. The request held the neuron’s 50 top contexts from the end of training (step 1,413,814), numbered 0 to 49, strongest first. Each context is the text around one token where the neuron fired: up to 256 tokens before that token and 16 after it, with the token marked ⟦ ⟧, and the activation there as a share of the neuron’s largest activation. The picture below shows a shorter piece of each context.

What it was asked to do

  • Put each of the 50 contexts in exactly one group, with one group for each thing the neuron responds to.
  • Group by what makes the neuron fire, not by topic alone. Contexts on different topics that are followed by the same word belong in one group.
  • A small offshoot of a group may become its own group, marked as related to it.
  • Do not make a new group just to hold one weak context.
  • Name each group, and say for every pair of groups whether they are related or unrelated.

One more rule comes from how these neurons are built. A neuron’s activation is the product of two numbers computed from the text, and it can fire when both of those numbers are negative. The essay treats such contexts as an echo of the neuron’s other side, not a job of their own, so the model put them in one separate group, and the study leaves that group out of every count (11 such groups in each sorting).

The answer is a list of named groups, each listing which of the 50 contexts it holds prompt infra/pg/neurons_prompts/clusterer.md.

Why there are two sortings

Asked the same question twice, the model can give two different answers. To measure how much, the study asked twice for every neuron: the same model, the same instructions and the same 50 contexts, with two independent answers. The first sorting is the first answer and the second sorting is the second.

The study uses the first sorting. The second sorting is a check on it, and it changes one thing: a group from the first sorting counts only if a single group of the second sorting holds more than half of its contexts. 116 of the first sorting’s 2,454 groups failed that test and were left out of the main result spec §8, B4; verdict q2.B4_unmatched_share.

Over the 600 neurons the first sorting made 2,454 groups, 4.1 per neuron, and the second made 2,025, 3.4 per neuron. The two counts were equal for 182 neurons and within one of each other for 407 (68%) verdict q2.B4_m_within_1; groups/README.md, checks.

The lithium neuron: 15 groups, then 3

The lithium neuron, L03N6372+, is the most extreme case. It is named for what it fired on earlier in training: at steps 60,000 and 320,000, 20 and 19 of its 20 strongest contexts were “ lithium”. By the end of training its 50 strongest contexts are other things: the second piece of a multi-word name (“Palo⟦ Alto⟧”, “Santa⟦ Cruz⟧”, “David⟦ Beckham⟧”), pieces of code and web addresses (“licenses⟦/MIT⟧”), and “baking⟦ parchment⟧”.

The first sorting split these 50 contexts into 15 small groups, mostly one per name: “Palo Alto” (5 contexts), “UC Santa Cruz” (4), “David Beckham surname” (3), “HuggingFace” (3) and eleven more. The second sorting made 3 broad groups: “second token of multi-token proper names and terms in prose” (28 contexts), “mid-identifier tokens in license URLs, tool and package names, code” (19) and “parchment after baking” (3). Each of the 15 small groups lies wholly inside one of the 3 broad ones. So the two answers agree on which contexts go together and differ in how finely they cut. No other neuron got more than 12 groups in the first sorting the sorting model’s record, L03N6372+.

The number of groups therefore depends partly on how finely the model chose to cut, not only on the neuron, and one neuron’s count should not be read on its own. The results below are reported over many groups together, and a later study compared these groups with groups built from the neuron’s own inputs, without reading any text (Are the groups in the neuron, or only in the text?).

The decisive readout: did the late groups grow from the early ones?

A group born after the neuron’s first group is late-born, and a judge tries to trace each late-born group to the neuron’s record from just before its birth (both pictures below). The share it cannot trace is 0 when it does as well as on control groups whose answer is known, and 1 when it does no better than a coin. The rule was written before any data: growth if the whole 95% interval lies below 0.10, packing if it lies above 0.20, mixed otherwise spec §2.4, §6.3.

What each result would mean

The four outcomes were written on 21 October, before any result; the examples are the got and lithium neurons in the pictures above.

If later jobs grow out of earlier ones

The got neuron is this case: each new group is a step from the last, and the judge traced each to the record before its birth in 4 of 4 calls A_training_dynamics.md §5. If most neurons with several groups looked like this, a neuron could be read directly, as the got neuron’s explanation is tested above, and the team’s next five studies would use the model’s own neurons.

If unrelated jobs are packed into one neuron

The lithium neuron is the nearest case: it lost its lithium top 20 to the second halves of famous names such as “Palo⟦ Alto⟧”, and the judge called 9 of its 12 late groups unrelated to the lithium record A_training_dynamics.md §2. Some name groups were already above its line during the lithium period (“Palo Alto” at step 60,000), so lithium may have sat on top of a name-completion neuron rather than been replaced E_skeptic.md §3.4. If most neurons looked like this, a description written from a neuron’s final contexts could not be trusted, and the team’s next five studies would keep decomposition methods.

If the result is mixed

This is what the rules written in advance reported: the share the judge cannot trace, 0.265, is above 0.20, which the rule calls packing, while the strongest group was among the first born more often than shuffled birth points give, which was read as growth already-clean RESULTS.md, Q2. On 5 November the second reading was corrected: random groups of the same sizes are first born even more often, so birth order gives no support to growth (below) next 10_RECOUNT2.md §7. The question then becomes when each account holds, which is why the later studies measured wiring.

If the judge fails its control

It did not: the judge was right on 91.0% of control calls on 39 tuning neurons before the 600 were judged, and on 92.4% on the 600 already-clean RESULTS.md, Q2.

What happened next, 24 October to 4 November 2026

The result of the training-point test, 24 October

By the rule written in advance the result is labelled packing: the share of the 1,238 late-born groups the judge could not trace is 0.265, with a 95% interval from 0.211 to 0.321, wholly above 0.20 already-clean RESULTS.md, Q2.

One other measurement was read as pointing toward growth; on 5 November a check showed that it does not. It asked whether a neuron’s strongest group at the end of training is also its first born. That holds in 273 of the 574 neurons with two or more groups, a share of 0.476 [0.436, 0.516], counting a tie for the earliest point as first born next 10_recount2/out/04_b.json; already-clean RESULTS.md, Retag. The rule written in advance compared this share with shuffling the birth points among each neuron’s groups, which exceeds 0.449 only 2.5% of the time, and so read the strongest group as the oldest more often than chance. That shuffle keeps each neuron’s real birth points and asks only which group gets which; it does not ask what birth point a group of the same size would get anyway. The fair comparison replaces each group with a random set of the same size drawn from the same neuron’s 50 strongest contexts, gives the strongest group’s random set the neuron’s top context, since holding it is what makes a group the strongest, and dates each set by the same rule. Random sets are first born in 0.530 [0.495, 0.568] of neurons, so the real 0.476 is below chance, not above it next 10_RECOUNT2.md §7; 06b_VERIFY_BIRTHS.md §5; 06_CRITIC.md Finding 0.2. Why random sets are first born so often is not settled: the three checks point to ties for the earliest point, to the strongest group’s size, and to its higher median activation. What still holds: the birth points follow the rule, 2,454 of 2,454 when recomputed from the saved activations, and some groups are dated beyond chance. Most groups, 2,115 of 2,428, fall within the range of birth points of their random sets, but 153 are born later than 95% of their random sets and 160 earlier, where chance gives 22.4 and 29.6 next 10_recount2/out/03_compare.json, 05_chance.json, 06_predictability.json. The births picture above now draws this chance level on every row.

Another measurement points toward growth: groups born before the strongest one that later shrank are rare, 0.035 [0.026, 0.044] against the 0.30 limit predicted already-clean RESULTS.md, Q2 grid.

Only 17 of the 600 neurons (2.8%) ended with a strongest group that was late-born and not linked by the judge to the neuron’s earlier record, where fewer than 30 had been predicted; if the strongest group is chosen by size instead of peak activation, 8 of the 17 remain already-clean RESULTS.md, Q2 grid; E_skeptic.md §3.4.

Of the five predictions, two came true (the shrinking groups and the 17), the audit prediction was right on one job and 1.5 points off on cannot tell (41.5% against 20% to 40%), and the two predictions about growth were wrong by the rules written in advance, in opposite directions already-clean RESULTS.md, §11 grid. Switching the old group’s inputs off, 1 November

That fall divided by the fall when the newer group’s own 50 are replaced is the reuse score reuse POST.md.

A finding in the saved data: the always-on neurons

Some neurons are active on nearly every token at levels far above the rest; the study took the 1% of earlier-layer neurons with the largest average activation, 3,412 neurons, and called them always-on. Inside a group’s own 50 main inputs they carry about 3% of the set’s effect on the neuron, but inside an unrelated neuron’s inputs about two thirds, so they inflate any measurement made by replacement reuse SPEC4_birth.md §0. The one cut takes 8% to 14% of the last layer and almost nothing below layer 20; the members that enter input sets are a few dozen neurons in layers 8 to 25 at 129 to 639 times their layer’s typical value reuse sanity/SANITY_science.md item 4.

Checking the tools themselves, 3 and 4 November

Every wiring number above rests on three untested choices: writing the average into each switched-off neuron, ranking a group’s main inputs by a one-pass estimate, and taking the groups from a language model’s sorting. The next study tested each against a known answer instruments SPEC_instruments.md; results/RESULTS.md.

Does the value you write in matter?

On the 56 fake neurons and the 150 real cases at the end of training, with the always-on neurons excluded, four values were written into the replaced neurons: the average, the median, zero, and a random draw from the neuron’s own record. Are the groups in the neuron, or only in the text?

For each of a neuron’s 50 strongest contexts, the list of how much each earlier neuron contributed is its input profile. Grouping contexts by profile, without reading any text, can be compared with the sorting model’s groups by the adjusted Rand index, which is 1 for identical groupings and 0 for chance; random groupings of the same sizes score below 0.06 in 95% of draws, and the sorting model agrees with itself at 0.65 instruments RESULTS.md Results 11, 12.

On 351 neurons the input profiles agree with the sorting model at 0.25 and grouping by marked token at 0.22; a balanced grouping method, chosen after that result was seen, reaches 0.44, a reading still to be confirmed on new neurons instruments RESULTS.md Results 12 to 14. A forecast that follows

For 277 neurons, the share of 50 main inputs that two input-defined groups hold in common predicts the fall of one when the other’s inputs are zeroed, with a correlation of 0.87, and a cheaper ranking that skips the contribution estimate predicts it at 0.81 instruments RESULTS.md Results 20, 21. The grouping rule was chosen on these same neurons, and two identical sets give a dependence of 1 by arithmetic, so the slope is partly built in.

Where the question stands, 4 November

Groups born in the first 4% of training are traced to the neuron’s past as reliably as the controls, as in the got neuron’s chain. Late-born groups, mostly on neurons whose groups all appear late, are traced less often, the overall untraced share is 0.265, and 17 of the 600 neurons ended with a strongest group that was late-born and not linked by the judge.

Underneath the text, a neuron’s groups are wired together: they share about an eighth of their main inputs, switching off one group’s inputs lowers the neuron on another by a quarter to a third of its own loss, and the overlap of the input sets predicts that dependence at 0.87. The dependence is the same for the oldest group as for any other, so a neuron’s groups use inputs they hold in common, whichever came first.

The birth of a group is a line crossing, not an event: the neuron already responds to the new group’s contexts before its birth point, and the response rises through the whole of training.

Writing zero, with the few dozen always-on OLMo neurons excluded, gives a reference near zero, and that is now the program’s rule. The sorting model’s groups follow the marked token closely, rarely splitting one token across groups, and the balanced grouping offers a way to define groups from the wiring instead.
