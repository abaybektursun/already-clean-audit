The first edition The files

Sixteen checkpoints

We followed individual MLP neurons of OLMo-3 7B through sixteen training checkpoints. This page reports each experiment in the order we ran it.

Interpretability work usually asks what a neuron responds to in the finished model. The Allen Institute for AI published 1,421 checkpoints of the first training stage of allenai/Olmo-3-1025-7B, so we could ask when a neuron starts to respond to something. We used sixteen checkpoints, from step 0 (random weights) to step 1,413,814 (the end of stage 1).

AI agents (Claude models) did the work for one person, who owns the project, set the rules, approved the spending and made the operating decisions. “We” means the agents. “The user” means that person. Before we closed a study, a second model recomputed its numbers from the saved files; we call it the reviewer.

Five results hold, one from October and four from November.

  1. At the end of stage 1, 51.8 % of the neuron poles we tested (95 % CI 47.8 to 55.8) respond to one recognizable kind of input. A published essay had claimed about 90 %.
  2. Training on a different data mix for 100 billion tokens changes less than one of a pole’s top-50 activating tokens.
  3. One checkpoint before a neuron starts to respond to a new cluster of inputs, we can pick that neuron out of the 22,016 poles of its layer. Our control, a random cluster from another neuron, picks out its neuron even more often.
  4. When a neuron starts to respond to a new cluster, its weights do not change more than a matched neuron’s.
  5. Reinforcement-learning fine-tuning raised the model’s MATH-500 score from 57.2 to 92.0. No set of 0.1 to 3 % of the neurons carries that gain.

In three November experiments the control beat our prediction (chapters 5, 8 and 9). Each time we report the control’s result. We also retracted one October result on 5 November: that a neuron’s largest cluster is usually its oldest.

Third edition, 10 November 2026: the second edition of 8 November rewritten with the field’s standard terms. The first edition of 6 November is kept unchanged under notebook.

·The terms, on one neuron

Every chapter uses six terms. Here is each one on a real neuron from our data.

Neuron. Each of the model’s 32 layers has an MLP with 11,008 neurons. At every token a neuron outputs one signed number, its activation. Our example is neuron 9,794 of layer 10.

Pole. A neuron’s positive activations and its negative activations usually respond to different inputs, so we study them separately. The essay we tested calls each one a pole, and we keep its word. L10.N9794+ is the positive pole of our example neuron. A layer has 22,016 poles.

Checkpoint. A checkpoint is the model’s weights saved at one training step. We name a checkpoint by its step: the checkpoint at step 320,000 is the Hugging Face branch stage1-step320000.

Top-50 activating tokens. We ran the final checkpoint over a fixed corpus of 20,560,068 tokens and kept, for each pole, the 50 tokens where its activation was largest, each with the text around it.

Cluster. A language model (Claude Sonnet 5.5) read a pole’s top-50 tokens, put the ones with similar content together and named each set. It split the top-50 of L10.N9794+ into four clusters of 19, 15, 12 and 4 tokens. The smallest is “access / CRUD in data-rights and privacy text”: the words “access”, “Access”, “access” and “CRUD” at four places in text about data rights and privacy. A cluster is a fixed list of corpus tokens, so we can compute the pole’s activation on those same tokens at any earlier checkpoint.

Onset. A cluster’s onset is the first checkpoint at which the pole responds strongly to the cluster’s tokens. The rule: the pole’s median activation on the cluster’s tokens reaches the pole’s own 99.9th-percentile activation at that checkpoint, and does so again at the next checkpoint. For the “access” cluster the median was 0.73 at step 180,000, below the percentile of 0.84. At step 320,000 the median was 0.85 and the percentile 0.67, and the median stayed above it at step 560,000. The onset is step 320,000.

When we say a cluster’s own pole, we mean the pole whose top-50 the cluster came from.

1Testing the essay’s claim

19 October to 25 October

Goodfire’s essay “Neural Networks Are Already Clean” claims that about 90 % of a model’s neuron poles do one job: they respond to one recognizable kind of input. We asked two questions. Does that hold on models nobody had checked? And when a pole responds to several clusters, did the later clusters grow out of the earlier ones (the essay’s account), or did training pack unrelated clusters into one neuron?

We drew 300 neurons at random from OLMo-3 7B and recorded the top-50 activating tokens of their 600 poles at each of the sixteen checkpoints. Claude Sonnet 5.5 clustered each pole’s top-50. A second model wrote an explanation of each cluster, and we scored the explanation on new texts. We dated each cluster’s onset. Before the run we wrote down our predictions: later clusters grow out of earlier ones, probability 0.65; a mixture, 0.30; unrelated clusters are packed in, 0.05.

strong: score ≥ 0.3153; pass: at least 3 of 5 positive texts strong, at most 2 of 5 near misses strong, at most 1 of 5 distant texts strong

Explanation scoring. A model reads only the explanation and writes five texts that should activate the pole, five near misses and five unrelated texts. A text’s score is the pole’s peak activation on it divided by the pole’s largest activation in the corpus. The explanation passes if the three counts above hold.

unrelated share = (control accuracy − A) / (control accuracy − 0.5); A = 0.812, control accuracy = 0.924, share 0.265

The parent test. For each cluster with a late onset, an LLM judge (Claude Sonnet 5.5) sees ten of the cluster’s tokens and two records of twenty top tokens from the checkpoint before the onset: one from the cluster’s own pole, one from a random pole of the same layer. The judge picks the record the cluster came from. If late clusters grow out of earlier ones, the judge should pick correctly; if they are unrelated, it can only guess. A is its accuracy. The control asks the same question about each pole’s largest cluster. We fixed the rule in advance: a lower 95 % bound above 0.20 means packing, an upper bound below 0.10 means growth, anything else a mixture.

The essay’s 90 % did not appear. On OLMo-3 7B, 51.8 % of poles had one job (95 % CI 47.8 to 55.8), and the two other models we tried gave 59.7 % and 56.3 %. We had predicted this in writing.

The parent test came out as packing, the outcome we had given 0.05. The unrelated share was 0.265 (95 % CI 0.211 to 0.321), and its lower bound is above 0.20, so the rule we had fixed in advance calls the result packing. Two tools failed before the final run. The first clustering model failed its agreement check (10 of 21). The first judge failed its control twice (0.673, 0.716), and we replaced it with a judge that passed at 0.910.

The packing result is weaker than its headline. We asked a separate model to attack the results, and it found that the judge’s control items were easier than its test items. When we re-weighted the control to match the test items, the unrelated share fell to 0.206 (95 % CI 0.132 to 0.271), and that interval includes 0.20. We withdrew our claim that training switches from growth to packing. On 25 October we found one defect in the model that wrote the explanations and one in the corpus pass. We did not re-run either, and we closed the study.

One result pointed toward growth, and we later retracted it. In 0.476 of poles the largest cluster also had the earliest onset. On 5 November we found that random clusters of the same sizes have the earliest onset even more often, in 0.530 of poles, so we retracted the result everywhere it appears.

Open: why random clusters so often have the earliest onset. The judge scored 0.798 on a harder control that required 0.80, so we cannot rule out that the judge only tells topics apart. Our plan said that after a packing result we should stop treating the neuron as the main object of study; no file records that we applied that rule or dropped it.

After the packing result we stopped comparing the clusters’ text and looked at the weights: clusters that are unrelated in content might still be fed by the same earlier neurons.

2Seven small studies

25 October to 4 November

Do a pole’s clusters share the earlier neurons that feed them? Does anything happen at a cluster’s onset? Do our editing tools measure what they claim to? We ran seven studies of a few GPU-hours each. None gave a result we would build on, and we report them briefly.

Single neurons. We ran three studies of single neurons, two on other models and one by hand on OLMo. None produced a valid result. In one comparison the validity check itself failed: zero-ablating a neuron did not remove its effect for 42 % and 37 % of poles (65 of 156 and 75 of 202).

Shared inputs. We scored how much the earlier-layer neurons that feed a pole’s newer cluster overlap those that feed its older cluster. Real poles scored 0.356. Fake poles, which we made by joining two unrelated neurons, scored 0.159. But the score barely changed when we computed it for a different neuron (0.332 against 0.339), so it may not measure the pole at all. We left it open.

Replacing inputs. We replaced the 50 earlier neurons that feed a pole’s older cluster with their mean activation and measured how far the pole’s activation on its newer cluster fell. We divided that fall by the fall from replacing the newer cluster’s own 50 inputs. The ratio was 0.226 (95 % CI 0.170 to 0.308) for real poles and 0.174 for fake poles at the final checkpoint. Under the rule we had fixed, the two do not differ, so the clusters do not reuse inputs. Against a baseline we added afterwards, fake poles measured at the onset checkpoint (0.031), they do.

Always-on neurons. While running that study we found 3,412 neurons with a large mean activation on ordinary text: the top 1 % of the neurons in layers 0 to 30, ranked by the absolute value of the mean. They account for about two thirds of the effect when we replace the inputs of an unrelated neuron. From 2 November we excluded them from the input-replacement method. An exploratory check found that they do not act through the layer’s normalization, and we withdrew an earlier sentence that said they did.

The onset. We found nothing abrupt at a cluster’s onset. One checkpoint earlier the pole already responded at 0.95 of its onset level (95 % CI 0.85 to 1.11). The activation rises through all of training. Its sharpest rise falls within one checkpoint of the onset in 0.307 of cases, against 0.283 at the 95th percentile of shuffled onsets. A pole did not depend more on its older cluster’s inputs than on another cluster’s (difference −0.046, 95 % CI −0.091 to 0.030).

A published weight edit. A paper edits the down_proj weights of 128 neurons of Qwen3.5-0.8B to stop the model repeating itself on mathematics problems. We asked whether those neurons are always-on neurons. 11 of the 128 are, against the 32 our rule required. We could not score the edit itself: 193 to 200 of 200 answers ran into the length limit.

Clustering without text. We clustered a pole’s top-50 tokens by which earlier neurons contributed to each, without reading any text. These clusters agreed with the LLM’s clusters at an adjusted Rand index of 0.25, where 1 is identical and 0 is random; the 95th percentile of random clusterings is 0.06. Clustering by the activating token alone agreed at 0.22, which we cannot separate from 0.25.

Editing tools against a known edit. We discarded every result. In the pilot, a share of 0.96 of the answers ran into the length limit, and that failed a check we had fixed in advance. We had also taken the edit’s direction from the published edit itself, so even the exploratory results say nothing about finding an edit from the weights alone.

fall = (a_before − a_after) / a_before; reuse ratio = fall when the older cluster’s 50 inputs are replaced / fall when the newer cluster’s own 50 are replaced

Input replacement. We take the 50 earlier-layer neurons that contribute most to a cluster and set each to its mean activation over ordinary text at every token. We read the pole’s activation on the newer cluster’s tokens before and after. The control does the same on a fake pole. The always-on rule removes the top 1 % of neurons by absolute mean activation (3,412) from the 50.

Input replacement must exclude the always-on neurons. We listed five open items (in the first edition) and six follow-up studies (studies/neurons/NEXT.md). 3Choosing the November experiments

5 November, 00:10 to 21:45

Which experiments were worth the money?

We wrote 29 ideas and had a critic model argue against each. At 01:35 we fixed five experiments, in this order: where the mathematics fine-tuning gain is stored (chapter 9); three data mixes (chapter 4); predicting a cluster’s neuron before the onset (chapter 5); whether the model holds an intermediate value in arithmetic (chapter 7); whether a cluster with an earlier onset costs more to ablate (chapter 10). The mechanism studies of chapters 6 and 8 came after them. We accepted an idea only if it had a before-and-after measurement, a prediction written before the measurement, or a number an engineer could use. We chose all five before any ran. Chapter 9 ran in parallel with chapters 4 to 8. In five places a result changed what we ran next, and chapter 11 lists them.

The critic’s strongest objection was to the October onset dates. It regrouped each pole’s top-50 at random into clusters of the real sizes. The random clusters gave a histogram of onsets close to the real one, and the real largest clusters had the earliest onset less often (0.476) than the random ones (0.530). An independent check confirmed the 0.476: it is below all 300 random draws. It also found that the two histograms are close but not equal, and that more clusters have a late onset than the critic had counted. We then recounted from the onset rule alone. 153 of 2,428 clusters have an onset later than 95 % of random clusters of the same size, where 22.4 would by chance. The recount traced much of the October result to ties for the earliest checkpoint; two other checks point to the largest cluster’s size and its higher median activation, so we have not settled the cause.

p = share of 2,000 random clusters with the same or a later onset; keep the cluster if p ≤ 0.05; 153 of 2,428 kept; even neuron index → development split (82), odd → held-out split (71)

For each cluster we draw 2,000 random clusters of the same size from the same pole’s top-50 and date each by the same rule. p is the share whose onset is at the same checkpoint or later. Chapters 5, 6 and 8 use only the 153 clusters with p ≤ 0.05. We set every threshold on the development split and then read the held-out split once.

4Three data mixes

5 November

Does the training data decide which documents a pole’s top-50 tokens come from?

AI2 trained three models for 100 billion more tokens from the stage-1 checkpoint at step 476,838. The three used different data: 0 %, 40 % and 65 % code and mathematics. For each of the 704,512 poles we counted how many of its top-50 tokens fall in the corpus’s five code-and-mathematics directories, before and after each mix, and averaged over bands of eight layers. Three checks passed before we looked at the result. We reproduced 1,535 of 1,535 saved activations bit for bit. We recorded the starting checkpoint twice and got identical counts. And we measured how much the count changes between consecutive stage-1 checkpoints, which is the change a mix has to exceed.

share(pole) = (top-50 tokens in the 5 code-and-mathematics directories) / 50; band mean over 8 layers; drift = the largest change of the band mean between consecutive stage-1 checkpoints (steps 320,000 → 560,000 → 1,000,000 → 1,413,814)

The test we registered: the 65 % mix minus the 0 % mix, paired by pole and averaged per band, must exceed the drift in every band, and the 40 % mix must lie between them by more than the drift. We computed the changes from the starting checkpoint, quoted below, afterwards. Recording the same checkpoint twice changes 0 of 704,512 poles.

The data mix moves a pole’s top-50 by less than one token. The 65 % mix minus the 0 % mix exceeded the drift in every band, by +0.011 to +0.020 of the share, which is half a token to one token of the 50. Measured from the starting checkpoint, the mix with no code or mathematics removed 0.6 to 0.9 of a token per pole from those directories, and the 65 % mix changed at most 0.35 of a token. We had predicted the order 0 % below 40 % below 65 %. The order holds against the bootstrap confidence intervals but not against the stage-1 drift, so we call it undecided. Our plan said to report this outcome and not to analyse single poles. The next experiment was already fixed: predicting a cluster’s neuron before the onset. On 8 November the reviewer corrected the size from “about one token of the fifty” to 0.6 to 0.9.

Open: whether a mix changes which kind of token dominates a pole’s top-50, a question we took from a preprint we read that afternoon. The measure needs two fixes before we reuse it.

5Predicting a cluster’s neuron one checkpoint early

5 November to 6 November

One checkpoint before a cluster’s onset, can we tell which neuron of the layer will respond to it? And does that work better for a real cluster than for a random set of tokens?

For each of the 153 clusters we took the checkpoint before the onset and scored all 22,016 poles of the layer by how strongly each already activates on the cluster’s tokens (the score is drawn in chapter 0). We recorded the rank of the cluster’s own pole. We set the thresholds on the 82 clusters of the development split and then read the 71 held-out clusters once. The control is a random cluster: tokens drawn from another pole’s top-50, the same number of them, dated to the same checkpoint by the same rule, and scored for that other pole.

Six runs were needed. Three stopped at a check that compared two GPU types. One crashed on an assertion. One stopped because the input that was built to fail a check could not fail it. The sixth is the run we report. We changed the GPU comparison from a stopping check to a reported number, because we could not set its threshold on control data. We also made a rule for every later experiment: before launch, run each check on saved control data and confirm that it can both pass and fail.

score(pole) = share of the pole’s own-sign tokens at the checkpoint before the onset with |a| below v; rank = number of poles in the layer with a score at least the own pole’s; hit: rank ≤ 10; by chance 10 / 22,016

v is the pole’s median activation on the cluster’s tokens at the checkpoint before the onset. A high score means the cluster’s tokens already sit near the top of that pole’s activations.

The cluster’s own pole ranks in the top ten of 22,016 in 42 of 71 held-out clusters, 1,302 times the chance rate. The control does better: a random cluster’s pole is in the top ten in 53 of 70. The difference is −0.171 (95 % CI −0.286 to −0.057). The saved examples show why the real clusters do worse: one checkpoint before the onset, other poles of the layer already respond to the same tokens. So we moved the next experiment forward: does the cluster’s own pole rise, or do the other poles fall (chapter 6)?

After seeing the data, the reviewer found where the gap comes from. In clusters that repeat one token, the own pole is in the top ten in 22 of 78 and the control in 53 of 78. In clusters with varied tokens the two are equal, 57 of 71 and 58 of 71. These counts pool both splits, 149 clusters. We registered a follow-up control matched on the number of distinct tokens.

Open, and taken up in chapter 6: whether the own pole rises or the others fall, whether the distinct-token control closes the gap, and whether the gradient one checkpoint earlier points at the own pole.

6The gradient, and a measurement that failed in bfloat16

6 November, 03:50 to 10:00

We asked three questions about the same 153 clusters. Does the own pole reach the top of its layer because its activation rises or because other poles’ activations fall? Does the distinct-token control close the gap of chapter 5? Does the loss gradient one checkpoint before the onset point at the own pole?

We would trust the gradient result only if a validity check passed. Gradient × activation predicts how much the loss changes when a pole is zero-ablated. The prediction had to agree with the measured zero-ablation at a Spearman correlation of at least 0.7. We had raised that threshold from 0.5 before the run.

We discarded the first run because its backward pass was not deterministic: 4 of 98 neurons changed between two identical runs. The second ran in bfloat16, with lower-precision attention so that it fit in the GPU’s memory.

own rise: F = log10(r_others) − log10(r_own); gradient at token i: s(i) = −sign · ∂L/∂a(i) · q; P = mean of s over the cluster’s tokens, P′ = mean over 16 control tokens matched on loss, S = P − P′

r_own is the own pole’s rank if only its own activation changes from the checkpoint before the onset to the onset checkpoint. r_others is its rank if only the other poles’ activations change. F above 0 means its own change gains it more rank than the others’ change does. sign is +1 for a positive pole and −1 for a negative pole. q is the pole’s 99.9th-percentile activation one checkpoint earlier, which puts poles of different scales on one scale. We wrote down five predictions before the run: the own rise; the distinct-token control; whether S is larger for the own pole; whether S one checkpoint earlier predicts which of the 20 top-scoring poles keep their rank; and the validity check.

We answered two of the three questions. The own pole reaches the top because its own activation rises (F = 0.951, 95 % CI 0.738 to 1.180); the reviewer found that the onset rule nearly forces F to be positive. The distinct-token control closes the gap: the own pole is in the top ten in 42 of 71, the control in 41 of 67 (difference −0.030, 95 % CI −0.149 to +0.090). We could not answer the third. The validity check failed, with Spearman correlations of 0.100 and 0.043 against the required 0.7, so we report the gradient results as exploratory.

At 08:10 the reviewer found that the measurement had failed, not the gradient. In bfloat16, the loss summed over a 2,048-token window is too coarse to register the loss change from zero-ablating one pole. The measured change was 27 times the predicted change at the median and unrelated to it. We withdrew our earlier explanation, that the first-order approximation breaks down. We decided to measure the zero-ablation in float32. Our plan had already named the experiment after that: how the own pole’s weights change at the onset. The same morning the user made a rule: a job that does not fit in the GPU’s memory runs on a larger GPU elsewhere, not at lower precision.

Open: whether the gradient one checkpoint earlier predicts which of the 20 top-scoring poles keep their rank (still undecided after chapter 8). We did not zero-ablate those 20 poles one by one, which would cost about one GPU-hour. Chapter 8 follows up both decisions; chapter 7 was already scheduled and ran first.

8The weights at the onset, and zero-ablation in float32

6 November to 8 November

We started two experiments together at 12:20. The first asked whether a neuron’s weights change more than usual when one of its clusters has its onset. We compared each cluster’s own pole with a matched pole: the pole of chapter 6’s distinct-token control cluster, dated to the same checkpoint. The second asked whether zero-ablation measured in float32 agrees with the gradient × activation prediction. If it does, we can interpret the gradient results of chapter 6.

Weights. A check that needed no GPU showed that the activation rises gradually: in 93 of 153 clusters, the step dated as the onset contains less of the rise than the two steps before it. So we measured the extra weight change over the three checkpoint intervals that end at the onset (chapter 0). The first run crashed. We stopped the second by hand and recorded that as a deviation from the plan. The third passed its 49 checks.

Zero-ablation. We computed the prediction in float32 with forward-mode differentiation and did not move to a larger GPU. That kept every measurement of the comparison on one GPU type and in float32. We noted that this departs from the user’s rule of the previous morning. We discarded the first run: in 535 of 963 ablated rows, the activations before the ablated token differed from the unablated run, which a causal model cannot produce. The second run passed that check.

Spearman correlation between predicted and measured loss change, over the (cluster, ablated pole) pairs of a split; permutation baseline = 97.5th percentile over 1,000 shuffles of the prediction (0.118); causality check: every ablated row bitwise equal to the unablated row before the ablated token

The weight measure e is drawn in chapter 0: the relative weight update between consecutive checkpoints, z-scored within the layer, averaged over the three intervals that end at the onset, minus its average over the earlier intervals. The headline is the mean of e(own pole) − e(matched pole) over 67 pairs, with a bootstrap confidence interval.

At the onset, the own pole’s gate_proj and up_proj rows change less than the matched pole’s, not more. The mean difference is −0.432 on the held-out split (95 % CI −0.817 to −0.059) and −0.217 on the development split (95 % CI −0.638 to 0.204). In plain units the own pole’s rows change 1.6 % less over the three intervals (95 % CI −3.2 to +0.03 %). Its down_proj column gives −0.258 (95 % CI −0.654 to 0.130). The own poles’ largest activations are 1.39 times the matched poles’; when we adjust for that, the interval includes zero. We concluded that the size of the weight change does not mark the onset. We did not test its direction.

In float32, measured zero-ablation agrees with the prediction at a Spearman correlation of 0.960 (95 % CI 0.915 to 0.990) on 284 held-out pairs. The bfloat16 and float32 predictions agree with each other at 0.995. So the failure in chapter 6 came entirely from summing the loss in bfloat16. With the measurement validated, we went back to chapter 6’s gradient results, as a dated re-analysis and not a prediction: the gradient one checkpoint before the onset is not larger for the own pole. We made a rule: compute first-order predictions in float32, with forward mode where the backward pass does not fit, and report a finite-difference check next to the zero-ablation.

Open: what does mark an onset in the weights. The next measurement we named is the projection of the row update onto the cluster’s direction, not its norm. After seeing the data we formed a hypothesis that the rise comes from a change in the residual stream that the pole reads. We also do not know which pole of a pair is the unusual one.

9Where the mathematics gain is stored

5 November to 8 November, in parallel with chapters 4 to 8

AI2 fine-tuned the finished model (allenai/Olmo-3-1025-7B, after training stages 2 and 3) with reinforcement learning on mathematics problems and published the result as allenai/Olmo-3.1-7B-RL-Zero-Math. Its MATH-500 score is 92.0 against the base model’s 57.2. Is that gain stored in a few neurons? We took the weight delta, fine-tuned minus base, copied parts of it into the base model, and scored each resulting model.

Seven things went wrong or changed before the conditions ran. Our grading code judged 108 of the 500 reference answers unequal to themselves; we fixed it before setting any threshold. We needed seven launches to reproduce a published score (39.96 against 40.0 ± 1.6). A trial run showed that no condition would fit under our 35 GPU-hour budget, and the user removed the budget. The first ranking showed that the weight delta is spread evenly over the layers, so we dropped the conditions that copied a window of layers. Our random-neuron control was meant to match the top neurons’ squared delta norm, but after rounding to bfloat16 it had only 0.70 to 0.82 of it; stochastic rounding fixed that. The reference runs gave: base 57.2; fine-tuned 92.0; base plus a zero delta 55.4; base plus the full delta 91.2. The gain of 34.8 points exceeded the 13.60 we required. Then the work stopped for 20 hours, during which two launches failed on an exact-equality check with nobody watching; we added a tolerance. After that the conditions ran. For each one we checked that the built model had exactly the intended weights and used the same generation software as the reference runs.

s(neuron) = sqrt(‖Δ gate_proj row‖² + ‖Δ up_proj row‖² + ‖Δ down_proj column‖²), ranked over all 352,256 neurons; DARE: keep each MLP entry of Δ with probability q = k / 352,256 and multiply it by 1/q; noise σ = 1.924 points from two seeds; two conditions differ if their scores differ by more than 5.44

A top-k condition copies the weight delta of the k % of neurons with the largest s and takes every other tensor from the base. A matched random condition copies random neurons scaled to the same total s². We read the model’s answer after its last “Answer:” line, or else from its last boxed expression, and grade it with a symbolic checker.

The neurons whose weights changed most do not carry the gain. Copying the top 0.1, 0.3, 1 and 3 % of neurons by delta norm gives 57.6, 61.4, 61.8 and 63.8; the 3 % condition recovers 6.6 of the 34.8 points. Random neurons with the same squared delta norm give 58.0 to 63.8, which is the same. DARE gives 85.2 while keeping a random 0.1 % of the MLP delta’s entries and multiplying them by 1,000. The full MLP delta gives 86.4, and the attention delta with the norm weights 83.0. On the 1,319 problems of the GSM8K test set, all three comparisons at 1 % came out the same way; the top neurons minus DARE is −23.7 points (95 % CI −26.5 to −21.1).

We state the conclusion in the wording the reviewer required: no 0.1 to 3 % subset of neurons carries the gain; a sparse unbiased estimate of the whole MLP delta does. DARE matches the top-neuron condition on the number of entries, not on norm: its squared norm is 33 to 1,000 times the full delta’s. So we do not know whether the gain needs the delta spread over many entries, or whether any scaled-up sample of it works. On 7 November we had proposed adding the top 1 % of neurons to another fine-tuned model. We withdrew the proposal on 8 November because those neurons do not carry the gain; only the handoff note records the withdrawal.

Open: what kind of weight change keeps working under this much noise. We named a test of the delta’s rank as the next measurement and have not specified or launched it. We could not determine whether the tolerance to noise is an artifact. The gap between the MLP delta and the attention delta comes from answers that hit the length limit. The small differences on GSM8K are fragile.

10Does an older cluster cost more to ablate?

6 November to 8 November

Take two poles of one layer, one whose cluster had an early onset and one whose cluster had a late onset, matched on activation size and on how often they fire. Remove the same amount of each pole’s output along its own down_proj column (chapter 0). Does the loss rise more for the older one?

We registered 74 matched pairs. After matching, onset age and activation size were uncorrelated (+0.027, from −0.592 before). The first run failed a check that recomputes each pole’s activations, because two batch sizes round differently. The second produced 45 balanced pairs and failed two checks that compute one quantity in two ways. A preview on 96 windows showed that the bootstrap spread was larger than the differences we wanted to detect. So we enlarged the evaluation set to 1,024 windows, added a power check to run first, cut the positive control to 8 layers, and split the pairs in two halves. Both halves waited for chapter 9’s GPUs, and the second would run only if the first passed the power check. We launched the first half on 7 November, before chapter 9 finished, so the GPU would not sit idle. The positive control ran inside it.

pair: two poles of one layer whose clusters’ onsets are at least 3 checkpoints apart, matched on ln max|a| (within 0.10) and ln firing rate (within 0.50); positive control passes if ablating along the pole’s own direction costs more loss than a random direction of the same norm in at least 6 of 8 layers; power: noise ≤ 0.5 × median |pair difference|

The noise is the median bootstrap spread, over evaluation windows, of a pair’s loss difference. We never read the second half of the pairs.

The experiment is invalid: its positive control failed and it lacked power. Ablating along a pole’s own direction should cost more loss than ablating along a random direction of the same norm. It did so in 2 of 8 layers, where we required 6. The random direction changed the residual stream 1.3 to 2.5 times more on every control pole, so the two ablations were not equal in effect. On the 21 pairs the two changed it equally (medians 60.8 and 60.7). The noise of a pair’s loss difference was 1.51e-6 nats per token, 1.186 times the median pair difference of 1.27e-6; we had required at most half of it, 6.36e-7. In an exploratory count the early-onset pole cost more in 16 of 21 pairs. We do not claim that.

On 8 November we decided to follow the rule fixed in advance and stop. Running the second half would add to a result the rule had already declared invalid, and a larger evaluation set is worth less than the rank test of chapter 9. The experiment leaves one design rule: match a control on its measured effect, not on its size in the weights.

Open: the question itself. No second reviewer recomputed the noise figure.
