The first edition The files

Sixteen snapshots

How the units of a language model changed during training, and what we tested, in the order we tested it.

A trained language model is a few billion numbers arranged in layers. In each layer of OLMo-3 7B there is an MLP (a two-layer feed-forward block) with 11,008 units, and every unit computes one signed number per token. The usual question about a unit is asked at the end of training: what does it respond to? The Allen Institute for AI (AI2) released 1,421 snapshots of this model’s first training stage; we used sixteen of them, from the random start to the end of the stage, so we could ask the question at every snapshot. This site is the record of what we asked, in the order we asked it, with every number drawn from a file.

The work was done by AI agents (Claude models) for one person, the project’s owner, who set the rules, approved the spending and made the operating decisions. On this page “we” is the agents, “the user” is that person, and “the reader” is a second model that re-derived each study’s numbers from its files before the study was closed.

Five results stand, one from October and four from November. About half of the sides we tested respond to one recognizable kind of input at the end of training (51.8 %, range 47.8 to 55.8). Changing the training data moves a side’s strongest contexts by less than one of its fifty. The side that will carry a new group of contexts can be named one snapshot before the group appears, and a random group of another side’s contexts names its own side even more often. The size of the carrier’s weight change at that moment is not the event. And when the finished model is taught mathematics, the gain is carried by no small set of units. Three times in November the control did better than our prediction (chapters 5, 8 and 9), and each time we report the control’s win as the result. One reading of the October data, that a side’s strongest group is also its oldest, was retagged on 5 November as not supported.

Second edition, 8 November 2026. The first edition of 6 November is kept unchanged under notebook. The first edition’s pictures appear in chapter 1; their text says neuron for unit and training point for snapshot.

·What the words mean

A unit is one of the 11,008 units in a layer’s MLP. At each token it computes one signed number, its activation. The tokens where that number is positive are the unit’s positive side; the tokens where it is negative are its negative side. Most measurements below are made on a side, because the two sides respond to different inputs. A snapshot is the model’s weights saved at one training step. A side’s fifty contexts are the fifty positions, in a fixed corpus of 20,560,068 scored tokens, where its activation was largest in its own sign at the final snapshot (most positive for the positive side, most negative for the negative side). A group is a set of those fifty that a separate model sorted together by shared content. A group’s birth is the first snapshot at which the side’s activation on the group’s positions crosses a threshold, defined in chapter 0, and stays above it at the next snapshot. The side whose group is born is the carrier. A job is a group that an explanation written from its contexts predicts on new text.

0What the operations are

Every chapter uses a handful of operations. Each is drawn here from the numbers in our files, with the true shapes and the exact rule. A reader who knows the operations can skip to chapter 1 and come back through the links.

1Fifty contexts, sixteen snapshots

19 October to 25 October

At the end of training, does a side respond to one recognizable kind of input? And if its fifty contexts fall into several groups, did the later groups grow out of the earlier ones, or did unrelated groups pack into the same unit?

A published essay from Goodfire (already-clean.goodfire.com) had claimed that about 90 % of a model’s units have one job. We harvested the sixteen snapshots for 600 sides of 300 units and recorded each side’s fifty contexts. A separate model sorted each side’s fifty into groups and named them. For each group a second model wrote an explanation and tested it on new texts. We dated each group’s birth by the rule in chapter 0. For each group born late, a judge model took the parent test: shown the group and two records from the snapshot before its birth, could it tell which record belonged to the group’s own side? Before the run we wrote down what we expected: growth from earlier groups 0.65, a mixture 0.30, packing 0.05.

strong: score ≥ 0.3153; pass: at least 3 of 5 positive texts strong, at most 2 of 5 near misses strong, at most 1 of 5 distant texts strong

The explanation test: five texts written from the explanation alone, five near misses and five distant texts are scored by the side’s peak activation on the text divided by its strongest activation in the corpus; the explanation passes only under the three counts above.

unrelated share = (control accuracy − A) / (control accuracy − 0.5); A = 0.812, control accuracy = 0.924, share 0.265

The parent test, drawn in chapter 0: A is the judge’s accuracy on late-born groups, and the control is the same question asked about a side’s main group against its own record; a share whose lower 95 % bound is above 0.20 was written down in advance as packing, an upper bound below 0.10 as growth, anything else as a mixture.

The results landed on the branches we had weighted least. On OLMo-3 7B, 51.8 % of sides had one job under an explanation that passed its test (range 47.8 to 55.8); the prediction we had written in advance, that the essay’s 90 % would not appear, held on all three models we tried. The parent test gave an unrelated share of 0.265 (range 0.211 to 0.321), its lower bound above the 0.20 line, so by the rule written in advance the result is packing, the branch we had given 0.05. Two tools failed before the run could count: the first model that sorted the contexts failed its agreement check, and the first judge failed its control twice before a second judge passed at 0.910.

One reading did point toward growth: a side’s strongest group at the end of training was also its first born in 0.476 of sides. On 5 November a check showed that random groups of the same sizes are first born more often, 0.530, so that reading is not supported and is marked so wherever it appears. A separate model, asked to argue against the results, found the judge’s control easier than its test items; re-weighting the control to the test items’ mix gives 0.206 (range 0.132 to 0.271), a sensitivity bound rather than a corrected estimate, and its range includes 0.20. We withdrew the claim that training switches from growth to packing (“phase change”). One defect in the model that wrote the explanations and one in the harvest were found on 25 October; neither was re-run, and the study closed.

Open: why random groups are first born so often; a harder control for the judge could not be measured (0.798 against the 0.80 required), so “the judge separates by domain alone” is not ruled out; and the plan’s own rule for a packing outcome, to keep the unit as one view among several rather than the object of study, which no file records as applied or set aside.

After the packing result we turned from the text of the contexts to the wiring: if a side’s groups did not grow out of one another in what they respond to, they might still share the units that feed them.

2Seven small studies

25 October to 4 November

If the groups were not growing from one another in the text, were they connected in the wiring? Was the moment of birth anything at all? And were our editing tools measuring what they claimed?

Seven small studies, each a few GPU-hours, told in order.

Three on single units, two in other models and one hand-run on OLMo, ended without a result that counted; one comparison was set aside when its own removal check failed for 42 % and 37 % of sides (65 of 156 and 75 of 202).

A wiring score, how far the earlier-layer units that feed a side’s newer group overlap those that feed its older group, read 0.356 against 0.159 for fake units made by joining two unrelated ones; but it hardly changed when computed for a different unit (0.332 against 0.339), and it was left open.

Replacing the older group’s 50 input units with their average lowered the unit on the newer group; the reuse score, that fall divided by the fall when the newer group’s own 50 are replaced, was 0.226 (range 0.170 to 0.308) against 0.174 for fake units at the end of training, so under the rule written first the reuse reading was refuted; against fake units measured at the birth, a reference added after the first reading (0.031), it read as reuse.

In the saved data we found the always-on units: the 1 % of units in layers 0 to 30 with the largest absolute mean activation over ordinary text, 3,412 of them, which carry about two thirds of an unrelated unit’s input effect. From 2 November they were excluded from the input-replacement method. An exploratory check found they do not act through the layer’s normalization (the planned rule could not be read), and an earlier sentence saying they did was withdrawn.

The moment of birth could not be read as an event: one snapshot before the birth the unit already responded at 0.95 of its birth level (range 0.85 to 1.11); the activation ratio rises through all of training, and the sharpest change sits within one snapshot of the birth in 0.307 of cases against 0.283, the 95th percentile for shuffled births. The older group was not preferred over another group of the same unit (−0.046, range −0.091 to 0.030).

In a published edit of another model, which changes the output weights of 128 of its units to stop it repeating itself on mathematics problems, 11 of the 128 edited units were always-on against a line of 32; the edit’s own test could not be read because 193 to 200 of 200 answers ran to the length limit.

Grouping contexts by their input profiles (for each context, how much each earlier-layer unit contributed), without reading any text, agreed with the groups the sorting had made at 0.25 on the adjusted Rand index (1 for identical groupings, 0 at chance), against 0.06, the 95th percentile of random groupings; grouping by the marked token alone agreed at 0.22, not separated from 0.25.

Last, the editing tools against that known edit: every reading was set aside because a share of 0.96 of the pilot’s answers ran to the length limit, failing a check fixed in advance; and since the direction had been taken from the published edit itself, even the exploratory readings show nothing about finding the edit from the weights.

fall = (a_before − a_after) / a_before; reuse score = fall when the older group’s 50 inputs are replaced / fall when the newer group’s own 50 are replaced

Input replacement: the 50 units of the earlier layers that feed a group most are each replaced by their mean over ordinary text at every position, and the side’s activation on the newer group’s positions is read before and after; the control does the same for a fake unit made by joining two unrelated units; the always-on cut removes the top 1 % of units by absolute mean activation (3,412) from the 50.

3One night: the choice

5 November, 00:10 to 21:45

Which questions were worth the money?

We wrote an inventory of 29 ideas and had a critic argue against each. At 01:35 the picks were fixed, in this order: where the mathematics gain lives; the three data mixes; naming the carrier before the birth; whether the model holds a variable; whether an older side costs more to edit; the mechanism studies after them. The rule for a pick: only an idea with a before-and-after measurement, a prediction made before the measurement, or an engineer’s number. All were chosen before any ran. Chapter 9 ran beside chapters 4 to 8: two tracks from one choice, not a chain in which each result chose the next. In five places a result did change what ran next; chapter 11 names them.

The critic’s strongest attack was on the October birth dates: random regroupings of each side’s fifty into groups of the real sizes gave a birth histogram close to the real one, and the real groups’ first-born share (0.476) was below the random reference (0.530). An independent check agreed on the first-born share (below all 300 random draws) but found the histogram close to, not equal to, the random one, and more groups dated beyond chance than the critic had counted. A recount from the rule alone found that 153 of 2,428 groups were born later than chance, against 22.4 expected; it traced much of the earlier reading to ties for the earliest date, while two other checks point to the strongest group’s size and its higher median, so the cause is not settled.

chance share ≤ 0.05 → the birth beats chance; 153 of 2,428; even unit index → rule-setting (82), odd → held out (71)

The chance share is the share of 2,000 random groups of the same size, drawn from the same fifty and dated by the same rule, that are born at the same snapshot or later; chapters 5, 6 and 8 use only the 153 births that beat it.

4Three data mixes

5 November

Does changing the training data move which documents hold a side’s strongest contexts?

AI2 trained three continuations of 100 billion tokens from one stage-1 snapshot: 0 %, 40 % and 65 % code and mathematics. For every one of the 704,512 sides we measured the share of its fifty strongest corpus positions that fall in the five code-and-mathematics directories, before and after each mix, paired by side, and summarized the mean over each band of eight layers. Before reading, three checks passed: 1,535 of 1,535 banked activations reproduced bit for bit; the drift between neighbouring stage-1 snapshots set the bar a difference had to clear; the start snapshot harvested twice gave byte-identical counts.

share(side) = (positions of the fifty in the 5 code-and-mathematics directories) / 50; band mean over 8 layers; bar = the largest change of the band mean between neighbouring stage-1 snapshots (12 to 13, 13 to 14, 14 to 15), by band

The registered test: the 65 % mix minus the 0 % mix, paired by side and averaged per band, must clear the bar in every band, and the 40 % mix must fall between them by more than the bar. The shifts from the start quoted below were read afterwards. The same snapshot harvested twice differs on 0 of 704,512 sides.

The registered contrast, the 65 % mix minus the 0 % mix, cleared the bar in every band (+0.011 to +0.020 of the share, about half to one of the fifty). Read from the start: the mix with no code or mathematics moved the share by 0.6 to 0.9 of one of the fifty per side, away from those documents; the 65 % mix moved it by at most 0.35 of one. The ordering we predicted, 0 below 40 below 65, held at the resolution of the resampling band but not at the resolution of ordinary stage-1 drift, so it is undecided. By the plan’s own decision table the next step for this outcome was to report, not to run a per-side analysis; the next study was already fixed as the third pick, naming the carrier before the birth. The size was corrected on 8 November, from “about one of the fifty” to 0.6 to 0.9, by the reader who checked the abstract.

Open: whether a mix moves which token family dominates a side’s strongest contexts, a reading prompted by a preprint we read that afternoon; two fixes to the measure before it is reused.

5Naming the carrier before the birth

5 November to 6 November

One snapshot before a group’s birth, can its carrier be picked out of its layer? And is the carrier easier to pick than a random side would be for a random group?

For each of the 153 births, we scored all 22,016 sides of the layer at the snapshot before the birth (the rule in chapter 0) and recorded the carrier’s rank. The rules were set on the 82 births of even-index units; the 71 births of odd-index units were then read once. The control was a random group of another side’s own fifty, of the same size, dated to the same snapshot by the same rule, scored the same way for its own side. Six attempts ran. Three stopped at a check that compared two devices; one crashed on an assertion; one stopped because the input that was supposed to fail its check had no power to; the sixth is the run reported here. The device check became a report rather than a stop: a threshold which cannot be set on control data is a measurement, not a check. A program rule followed: the offline check evaluates both sides of every check on banked controls before launch.

score(side) = share of its own-sign tokens at snapshot k − 1 with |a| below v; rank = sides of the layer with a score at least the carrier’s; in the top ten: rank ≤ 10; chance 10 / 22,016

v is the median activation over the group’s positions at k − 1; the control group is a random group of another side’s fifty of the same size and layer, dated to the same snapshot by the same rule, scored for its own side.

The carrier is named at 1,302 times chance, and the control is named more often: the difference is −0.171, range −0.286 to −0.057. The raw examples showed why: other sides of the layer already held the group’s positions one snapshot before. The next step: the study of whether the carrier rises on its own or the other sides fall (chapter 6) moved up. The reader of this study found, after the data were seen, that the gap sits on births whose contexts repeat one token and not on births with varied tokens (over both halves, 149 births with a control: the carrier named in 22 of 78 repeated-token births and the control in 53 of 78; 57 of 71 and 58 of 71 for varied-token births); a control matched on the number of distinct tokens was registered as the follow-up.

Left open, and taken up next: does the carrier rise on its own or do the others fall; does the distinct-token control close the gap; does the gradient one snapshot earlier point at the carrier.

6The gradient, and a measurement that could not resolve

6 November, 03:50 to 10:00

Three questions on the same 153 births, and a condition: the gradient’s answer would count only if a first-order prediction of switching a side off agreed with the real switch-off at rank agreement 0.7, a bar raised from 0.5 before the run.

The first attempt was set aside because the backward pass was not bit-deterministic (4 of 98 units flipped). The second ran in 16-bit, with reduced-precision attention to fit the GPU’s memory.

own rise: F = log10(r_others) − log10(r_carrier); gradient pull at a position i: −sign · ∂L/∂h(i) · q; P over the group’s positions, P′ over 16 loss-matched positions, S = P − P′

r_carrier is the carrier’s rank when only its own activation moves from the snapshot before the birth to the birth snapshot, r_others its rank when only the other sides’ activations move; F above 0 means its own move buys more rank than the others’ move does. sign is +1 for a positive side and −1 for a negative side; q is the side’s 99.9th-percentile activation one snapshot earlier, so sides of different scales share one unit; the 16 loss-matched positions lie outside the group and match its positions on loss. Five readings were written before the run: whether the carrier’s rank rises or the 20 sides that scored highest fall; whether the distinct-token control closes the gap; whether S singles out the carrier; whether the pull one snapshot earlier foretells which of the 20 keep their rank; and the validation that the first-order prediction of switching off tracks the measured switch-off at rank agreement 0.7.

Two of the three questions were answered. The carrier wins by its own rise (0.951, range 0.738 to 1.180), and the reader found that the dating rule nearly forces that sign. The distinct-token control closes the gap: carrier in the top ten in 42 of 71, control in 41 of 67 (difference −0.030, range −0.149 to +0.090). The third was set aside: the validation failed, and the gradient readings were printed as exploratory.

The reader’s finding at 08:10 changed what we thought had failed. In 16-bit, the loss summed over a 2,048-token window cannot resolve a change as small as one side’s switch-off; the check had failed on that, not on the gradient. The measured change was 27 times the prediction at the median, with no relation to it. An earlier reading, “first-order breakdown”, was withdrawn. The decision: build the switch-off measurement in 32-bit. The study’s own decision table had already named the next object: the weight change behind the carrier’s rise. The user’s rule the same morning: a job whose memory exceeds the GPU runs on a larger GPU elsewhere, not under a precision compromise.

Open: whether the pull one snapshot earlier foretells which of the 20 top-scoring sides keep their rank (undecided after the re-read in chapter 8); a switch-off of each of those 20 sides, about one GPU-hour, not run. Chapter 8 takes the two decisions up; chapter 7, already scheduled, ran first.

8Two measurements at once

6 November to 8 November

Two studies were started together at 12:20. One asked whether the carrier’s weights change more at the birth than those of the side named by chapter 6’s distinct-token control group, dated to the same snapshot (the matched unit, below). The other asked whether the 32-bit switch-off agrees with the prediction, so that the gradient readings of chapter 6 could be re-read.

For the weights, a pre-check at zero GPU showed that births are gradual (in 93 of 153 the dated step holds less of the rise than the two before it), so the statistic became the excess change over the three steps ending at the birth (chapter 0). The first attempt crashed, the second was stopped by hand and ruled a deviation, the third passed 49 checks. For the switch-off, a forward-mode 32-bit prediction was chosen over a larger GPU because it fit the same GPU type as every other measurement in the comparison, in 32-bit throughout, and needed no new machine image; the record notes this against the user’s rule of the previous morning. The first attempt was set aside: 535 of 963 switched-off rows were not bitwise equal to their baseline before the switched position. The second passed.

rank agreement = the rank correlation of prediction and measurement over a half’s pairs; reference = the 97.5th percentile over 1,000 shuffles of the prediction (0.118); causal check: every switched-off row bitwise equal to its baseline before the switched position

The weight statistic e is drawn in chapter 0; its headline is the mean over 67 pairs of e(carrier) − e(matched unit), with a resampling band.

The carrier’s input rows changed less, not more: −0.432 on the held-out half, −0.217 (range −0.638 to 0.204) on the rule-setting half, and in natural units −1.6 % of the rows’ own change over the three steps ending at the birth (range −3.2 to +0.03 %). The output column read −0.258 (range −0.654 to 0.130). Carriers’ largest activations are 1.39 times the controls’, and adjusting for that widens the range to cross zero. The decision: the size of the carrier’s weight change is not the event; the direction of the change is untested.

The 32-bit measurement agreed with the 16-bit prediction at 0.960, and the two predictions agreed with each other at 0.995: the 16-bit resolution of the summed loss had been the whole problem. The chapter 6 gradient readings were then re-read, as a dated re-reading and not a prediction: the gradient one snapshot earlier does not single out the carrier. A rule was written: compute first-order predictions in 32-bit, forward mode where the backward pass does not fit, and report the finite-difference check beside the zero switch-off.

Open: what a birth is, beyond the size of the weight change (a projection of the row change on the group’s direction, not a norm, is the named next measurement); whether the rise is carried by a change in the shared vector the carrier reads, a hypothesis formed after the data; which side of a pair is the unusual one.

9Where the mathematics gain lives

5 November to 8 November, alongside chapters 4 to 8

The first pick, closing the track that ran beside chapters 4 to 8. When the finished model is trained further by reinforcement learning on mathematics problems, rewarded for right answers, is the gain in a few units?1

The offline check found that the grading rule judged 108 of the 500 reference answers unequal to themselves; it was fixed before the rules were set. The published score took seven launches to reproduce (39.96 against 40.0 ± 1.6). A smoke run showed no condition would fit under the 35 GPU-hour ceiling, and the user removed the limits. The first ranking run showed the change was flat across layers, so layer-window conditions were retired. The random units meant to match the most-changed units’ energy realized only 0.70 to 0.82 of it after 16-bit rounding; stochastic rounding fixed it. The reference run read base 57.2, mathematics model 92.0, zeroed change 55.4, identity 91.2; the gain of 34.8 cleared the bound of 13.60. A 20-hour outage followed, and two launches failed on an exact-equality check while nobody was watching; a tolerance was added. Then the conditions ran, each checked to use exactly the intended weights and the same generation software as the reference run.

s = sqrt(‖Δgate row‖² + ‖Δup row‖² + ‖Δdown column‖²), ranked over 352,256 units; scaled sample: keep each MLP entry with probability q = k / 352,256 and multiply by 1/q; σ = 1.924 from two seeds; a difference counts above 5.44

The most-changed conditions take the top share of units by s with every other tensor from the base; the equal-energy conditions take random units scaled to the same total s²; the answer is the text after the last “Answer:” line, else the last boxed expression, graded by a symbolic checker.

The most-changed 0.1, 0.3, 1 and 3 % of units read 57.6, 61.4, 61.8 and 63.8; the 3 % recovers 6.6 of the 34.8 points. Random units of equal change energy read 58.0 to 63.8, the same. A random 0.1 % of the MLP’s entries, scaled up to estimate the whole change, read 85.2. All MLP read 86.4; attention with every norm weight 83.0. On 1,319 held-out word problems the three comparisons at 1 % all replicated; the most-changed units minus the scaled sample read −23.7 (range −26.5 to −21.1). The decision, in the words the abstract’s reader made us use: no 0.1 to 3 % subset of units carries the gain; a sparse unbiased estimate of the whole MLP change does. The scaled sample is matched on count, not on energy (its energy is 33 to 1,000 times the change’s), so whether the gain needs the change spread over many entries, or only a scaled-up copy of any sample of it, is open. A proposal, made on 7 November, to add the most-changed 1 % to another post-trained model was withdrawn on 8 November, because the most-changed units did not carry the gain; the withdrawal is recorded in the handoff and nowhere earlier.

Open: what shape of change the network tolerates such noise on (a rank test is the named next measurement, not yet specified or launched); whether the noise tolerance is an artifact (indeterminate); the MLP-against-attention gap sits in answers that hit the length cap; the small word-problem contrasts are fragile.

10Does an older side cost more to edit?

6 November to 8 November

The fifth pick. Take two sides in one layer, one born early and one born late, matched on activation size and density. Remove the same amount from each side’s write along its own column, all of the smaller one and the matching amount of the other (chapter 0). Does the older one cost more in loss?

The study pre-registered 74 matched pairs; after matching, age and activation size were uncorrelated (+0.027, from −0.592 before). The first attempt failed a check that recomputes each side’s activations, because two batch sizes round differently; the second gave 45 balanced pairs and failed two checks that compare the same quantity computed two ways. A power preview at 96 windows showed the resampling spread exceeded the contrasts, so the evaluation set went to 1,024 windows, a power check was added and paid first, the positive control was trimmed to 8 layers, and the run was split in two halves, both queued behind chapter 9’s runs, the second to run only if the power check on the first passed. The first half launched on 7 November, before chapter 9’s result, rather than hold the GPU idle; the positive control ran inside it.

pair: two dated sides of one layer, births at least 3 snapshots apart, matched on ln max|a| (within 0.10) and ln density (within 0.50); control passes if the own-direction edit costs more than the random edit in at least 6 of 8 layers; power: noise floor ≤ 0.5 × median |pair difference|

The noise floor is the median resampling spread of a pair difference over windows; the second half of the pairs was never read.

The control ran backwards. The own-direction edit cost more than the random edit in 2 of 8 layers, where 6 were needed, and the random edit moved the shared vector 1.3 to 2.5 times more on every control side; on the 21 pairs the two edits moved it alike (medians 60.8 against 60.7 of the shared-vector change R). The power check failed too: the noise of a pair difference, 1.51e-6 nats per token, was 1.186 times the median pair difference (1.27e-6), where it had to be at most half of it (6.36e-7). An exploratory reading exists (the early side’s edit cost more in 16 of 21 pairs) and is not a claim. The decision on 8 November: the rule fixed in advance decides; the second half would buy a result that rule had already set aside; a larger evaluation set is lower leverage than the rank test of chapter 9. The design rule the study left, recorded as a finding: match controls on their effect, not on their size in the weights.

Open: the question itself, whether an older side costs more to edit; the noise floor, which no second reader recounted.
